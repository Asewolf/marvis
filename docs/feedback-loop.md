# The feedback loop

This is the part that actually changes behaviour, and it is the reason to bother with any
of this.

## The pattern

Something goes wrong. You correct it. **The correction becomes a file.**

Not a mental note, not a line in a chat that scrolls away. A file, in `memory/`, typed
`feedback`, that loads through the index in every future session.

```markdown
---
name: never-send-without-approval
description: Draft, show, wait. Never send anything on my behalf without an explicit go
metadata:
  type: feedback
---

Nothing goes out without an explicit "send" from me. Emails, form submissions, posts,
anything a third party sees.

**Why:** It sent a client email with the wrong figure in it. Drafts cost nothing to
review. A sent email cannot be recalled.

**How to apply:** Draft it, show me the full text, stop. A question from me is not
approval. Re-show it if it changed after I approved it.
```

## Three parts, and all three matter

**The rule**, stated plainly enough to follow without interpretation.

**The why.** This is the part people skip and it is the most valuable. A rule with a reason
survives contact with an edge case the rule never anticipated. A rule without one either
gets applied stupidly or gets abandoned the moment it looks inconvenient.

**How to apply.** Where it bites, what it does not cover, what the exception is.

## Write the confirmations too

Feedback is not only corrections. When the assistant does something well and you want that
to become the default, that is worth a file as well. "When I am overwhelmed, give me three
bullets and one action" is as useful as any prohibition.

## When a correction contradicts an old one

Update the existing file. Do not write a second one. Two files disagreeing is how you get
behaviour that flips depending on which one was read first.

If the old memory was simply wrong, say so in the file. A note saying it was corrected on
a given date and the earlier version was wrong stops anyone, human or assistant, from
reconstructing the mistake later.

## What this feels like after a month

The first week you write a lot of these. The second week, fewer. By the second month the
assistant already knows the answer to most of what used to annoy you, and the files that
remain are the genuinely subtle ones.

That is the whole return. The correction you make once is the one you never make again.
